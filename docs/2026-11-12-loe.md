# Level of effort: converting the book to §1.7 and §4.2b

12 November 2026. Written in answer to *"if the LOE to get them all there is low let's do
it but otherwise let's go 1 at a time."*

**Short answer: it is not low. Go one at a time.** ~11–15 sessions in total, and the spread
between the cheapest chapter and the dearest is a factor of three. The recommended first
chapter is **the Murmuration with its roost**, for reasons that are about what it would
*teach*, not about its size.

**One caveat that applies to every "lit" number below.** The Free Dive has still never been
rendered (the push is blocked — see `HOW-TO-PUSH.md`). Every lighting estimate here assumes
`js/light.js` works roughly as written. If it does not, all of them move.

---

## 1. The two halves are not equally dear, and they are not independent

### The reader-led half (§1.7) is cheaper than it looks

Three chapters already read a continuous finger: Long Air, the Shepherd and the Murmuration
each carry an identical `reachAt` — *"0..1 down the screen, or null when the reader is not
steering"* — and already pursue it. Pearl Diver and Bamboo Rain have `pointerdown` only: a
tap, not a position. So two of the five need a control path built from nothing and three
need one re-pointed.

What is missing in all five is the same two things: nothing measures the reader's *rhythm*
(there was no period tracker anywhere in the book before `js/lead.js`), and every world
structure is still derived from `B.value(t)` or from the protocol's cycle length.

**And there is a cheap trick available for the two travelling chapters.** Long Air's course
and the Shepherd's hills are fixed periods in *distance*, not in time. The reader's rate can
therefore enter as **forward speed** — drive speed from `superstructure().period` and the
baked geometry survives untouched. The gate grid stays where it is; the glider simply
reaches the next gate when the reader's swell says so. This is what makes those two
tractable at all, and it is also exactly §1.7's "the offering should be there in the
environment": the superstructure runs 8% slow, so the gates arrive a fraction after you do.

### The lit half (§4.2b) is dearer than it looks, and the reason is normals

The discriminator per chapter is whether its surfaces have a usable normal.

- **The Free Dive's seabed was designed for this.** `bedAt` is sums of sines and one
  smoothstep, and its own comment says why: *"its normal is its analytic gradient and the
  mesh can be lit without a single faceted band."* Nothing else in the book was built to
  that standard, because until today nothing needed to be.
- **The Shepherd is fine.** `groundAt` is sines, an exp and one rational — differentiable by
  hand in twenty minutes.
- **Long Air's ridge is the blocker, and it is a structural one.** `terrainY` interpolates
  between columns with a *quintic ease*, chosen because it "cannot overshoot and has zero
  slope at every knot". Zero slope at every knot is precisely what you must not have under a
  light: each column becomes a flat spot joined to the next by a steep middle, i.e. a rib,
  every 1-ish metre across the surface that fills the frame. Unlit this is invisible and the
  choice was correct. Lit it is the whole picture. Fixing it means changing the
  cross-section interpolation, which changes the silhouette, which reopens the projected
  sagitta bar and the column budget that three passes have already been spent on.

**And one cost lands on the whole repo rather than on a chapter.** CLAUDE.md's own Shepherd
note says it: *"An unlit surface hides its own facets… what shows a row boundary is the edge
where the ground meets the SKY."* Every continuity bar in this repo was derived under that
assumption. Lighting makes **interior** knots visible, so those bars have to be re-derived
against a shading criterion rather than a silhouette one, per chapter, as each is converted.
`tools/continuity.mjs` is 1,504 lines and measures kink angles it currently reports as
cosmetic.

### The gate is a shared cost, paid once, with the first conversion

`tools/regress.mjs` is 1,533 lines with 78 references to the schedule, and its headline
guarantee — "on-pace play never touches a surface" — is explicitly a promise about a reader
conforming to *our* rate. For a reader-led chapter it means nothing. `tools/lead.mjs` is the
replacement pattern (properties over a family of reader behaviours, pathological ones
included) and it cost 303 lines to cover **one** chapter. Budget **1–1.5 sessions** of
shared scaffolding, best spent alongside whichever chapter goes first.

### Item (3) partly dissolves, and that is good news

The leftover old-chapter work — Bamboo Rain and the cover column having no anti-aliasing
pass — is mostly *subsumed* rather than separate. Hard posterised risers are the thing that
needed an AA pass; §4.2b permits gradients, so Bamboo Rain's three `rampPick` sites and
`deep-light-idle.js`'s two become lit shading and the aliasing goes away as a side effect of
the conversion. Do not budget it separately.

The other leftover goes the other way. Long Air's ridge still uses `scrollGlsl`, whose
stretched quad is currently *masked* — CLAUDE.md records it as looking like more canyon
because the cross-section barely varies along z. Under a light, a 216 m flat ribbon with a
hillside rising through it will not look like canyon. **Lighting Long Air forces the
scroll fix**, which the Shepherd's own experience prices at roughly +50% triangles.

## 3. Recommended order, and the reason

**1. The Murmuration, with the roost.** Not because it is cheapest, though it is. Because:

- It is the only chapter that is currently **broken by its own diagnosis**. The others work.
- It has no terrain, so it is the one conversion where the lighting cost is honest and small
  and nothing in the continuity machinery is disturbed.
- **It is the cheapest honest test of whether the superstructure is legible at all** — which
  is the one open question the whole of §1.7 rests on. The Murmuration is the standing
  cautionary case: its numbers were good and the chapter did not read. If a coupled
  superstructure is not *perceptible* to a reader, that finding arrives after 2 sessions
  instead of after 6.
- And its own fix and §1.7 pull the same way. The roost diagnosis says the quantity must
  become *a count of birds in the air* against *a fixed reference in shot*. A count is
  already reader-led in form: it is not a rate, it is a state the reader puts the world into.

**2. The Shepherd.** It proves the two patterns the other conversions need — lighting a
procedural terrain cheaply, and speed-from-superstructure on a fixed spatial period — on the
chapter where both are easiest.

**3. Bamboo Rain *or* Long Air**, decided by what the first two teach. Bamboo Rain needs a
design answer first; get it written down before it is scheduled.

**4. Long Air last, deliberately.** It is the chapter the reader is happy with at 58–60 fps.
It therefore has the most to lose and the least to gain, and by the time it is reached the
pattern will exist and the cross-section question will have been answered somewhere cheaper.

**If Long Air is wanted sooner,** spend one session on a spike that answers only this: does
a lit cross-section need re-knotting, and what does that do to the column budget? That one
number decides whether Long Air is a 4-session job or a 6-session one, and it can be had
without committing to either.

## 4. What would make this a batch job after all

Two findings would collapse the estimate, and both are cheap to test:

1. **If `rampPick` → lit shading is close to mechanical** on a surface that already has
   normals, the lit half of Bamboo Rain and the cover column is hours, not a session. The
   Shepherd conversion will show this.
2. **If the superstructure proves legible on the Murmuration**, the §1.7 half of every
   remaining chapter becomes a known pattern rather than an open design question, and the
   control-path estimates above are conservative by roughly half.

Neither is knowable before the first conversion, which is the real argument for doing one.
